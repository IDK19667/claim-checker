How Evident reaches a verdict

Every result is built the same way, in this order.

  1. Your claim becomes a search. An AI model turns the claim into the terms researchers use, and Evident searches PubMed, the U.S. National Library of Medicine's index of medical research, for the most relevant records. If nothing matches, it tries once more with a broader search.
  2. It reads up to {{ max_studies }} abstracts. An abstract is the summary at the top of a paper. Evident reads the summary, not the full paper.
  3. Each study is sorted by its design, from PubMed's own labels, and by whether it was run in people or in animals or cells. Reviews that pool many studies and randomized trials count most; single case reports and opinion pieces count least.
  4. The AI model writes the verdict from those abstracts only, and must name the studies it relied on.
  5. The code checks the result. A citation must be one of the studies the model was shown. A verdict that cites none, or rests only on case reports or animal and lab studies, becomes “{{ labels.insufficient }}”. A sentence that leans on a study the verdict did not rely on, or gives a figure or a group of people its cited study does not mention, is removed before you see it. A warning about harm is kept.
  6. Every result says what is still open: who the studies left out, how short they ran, or what finding would change the answer.

On claims where a wrong answer can hurt someone, such as medicines, vaccines, infections, cancer, pregnancy, children or stopping a treatment, the AI model is also asked what major health bodies such as the WHO, the CDC and the NHS advise. When that advice is plain and the verdict disagrees, the verdict follows it, and the short answer begins “Major health guidelines say:”. That is the model's account of the advice, not a quote from those bodies.

How sure it is

The line under the evidence chart says how strong the evidence is, from the designs of the studies the verdict relied on:

  • Strong: it rests on a meta-analysis, a systematic review, a randomized trial or a practice guideline. A review that pools only observational studies counts as moderate.
  • Moderate: other clinical trials, observational studies or narrative reviews, or a study PubMed gives no design for.
  • Weak: only case reports, letters, editorials or comments.

When a review rates its own evidence, for example with GRADE or CINeMA, its words replace that one: high, moderate, low or very low certainty. The label is never higher than the lowest rating any review it relied on gives, and never higher than any certainty the result's own text states. It drops to low certainty when the main review rates its evidence low or puts the link down to other factors, or, with no review behind it, when every study is small, a pilot, paid for by the maker or has no group that went without.

“{{ labels.false }}” needs studies that contradict the claim. A claim nothing tests in people is “{{ labels.insufficient }}”, never false. “{{ labels.complicated }}” is for real evidence that is mixed, partial or uncertain.

What Evident cannot do

  • It reads summaries, and a summary can leave out or overstate a result.
  • It reads at most {{ max_studies }} studies, so it can miss one, including a newer one.
  • No doctor or scientist checks each result. Mistakes happen: report one with the button under the result.
  • It does not know your health. It is not medical advice.
