          Before you trust a verdict

          Where do the studies come from?

          From PubMed, the U.S. National Library of Medicine's index of medical research, with more than 37 million records. A new check searches it and reads the abstracts of up to 8 of the closest matches. Every study it read is listed under the verdict, with a link to its PubMed page.

          Is an AI making this up?

          An AI model{% if provider != "an AI model API" %} ({{ provider }}){% endif %} reads those abstracts and writes the verdict. It can only cite studies it was shown: the code checks every citation against that list. A verdict with no citations, or one resting only on case reports or animal and lab studies, is turned into “{{ labels.insufficient }}” before you see it. It can still misread an abstract, which is why every study it used is one tap away.

          Is it biased? Who made it?

          Evident is an independent project. It shows no ads, has no accounts and sells no data, so no verdict makes it money. Its rules lean towards caution: “{{ labels.complicated }}” and “{{ labels.insufficient }}” are ordinary answers here, and a confident one needs studies behind it. Research has blind spots of its own, so every verdict also says what is still open. Who runs Evident

                How a check works

                1. Your claim becomes a search. An AI model turns "apple cider vinegar cures diabetes" into the terms researchers actually use.
                2. PubMed answers. That's the U.S. National Library of Medicine's index of medical research, 37 million papers. We take up to 8 of the best matches with their abstracts.
                3. The studies get weighed, not counted. A meta-analysis of many trials outranks one small pilot. A case report about one patient barely counts. The model reads the abstracts and says which studies it relied on.
                4. The verdict is stamped, with rules. "False" needs studies that contradict the claim. Nothing that tests the claim, or only single case reports and lab work, means "not enough evidence," never a confident answer. "It's complicated" is for real evidence that is mixed or uncertain. Studies that only match the words don't count.
                5. Every verdict says what's still open. Research is rarely finished. The last line names the gap: who the studies left out, how short they ran, or what finding would change the answer.

                Reading the evidence meter

                • Strong designs: the verdict rests on a meta-analysis, systematic review, or randomized trial.
                • Moderate designs: human studies or expert reviews, but no randomized trial behind it.
                • Weak designs: only case reports, letters, or opinion pieces.
                • No direct evidence: nothing found actually tests the claim.
                • Low certainty: the review behind it rates its own evidence low, or every trial is small or a pilot.

                The How sure line says whether the studies reviewed support the claim itself, then how sure or what kind of study it rests on. A strong design that finds a small effect is not support for a cure.

                The full method, and what Evident cannot do

                Not medical advice. The model can misread an abstract, and PubMed doesn't index everything. Read the studies. Talk to a clinician about your own health.
